1.0 introduction
Livingstone City is Zambia’s premier tourist destination and a strategic transit hub in Southern
Province. With an estimated resident population of approximately 177,393 people and a large
transient and tourist population, the city’s exposure to climate risks has implications not only
for local development, but also for national economic performance, public health, and
international reputation. Similar to most parts of the country, Livingstone experiences climate-
induced changes to physical and biological systems which are already exerting considerable
stress on the vulnerable sectors. Agriculture and food security, wildlife, forestry, water and
energy, health and infrastructure have been adversely impacted, thereby affecting the
economic, social, and environmental dimensions of sustainable development efforts (Irish
Aid,2028).
Livingstone is characterised by a hot and humid climate, increasing heat extremes, variable
rainfall patterns, a low water table, and predominantly sandy soils with low water retention
capacity. These physical conditions, combined with rapid urbanisation, environmental
degradation, and service delivery constraints, increase the city’s vulnerability to climate
change.
This Climate Change and Resilience Strategy provides a structured, evidence-informed, and
locally appropriate roadmap in contributing to achieving the City’s vision of being “A Tourism
Destination with diverse socio-economic developmental activities driven by local participation
and inclusive governance, ensuring sustainable growth and development.” In line with the
integrated development plan, while also ensuring that it aligns to the eight national
development policy and the national policy on climate change objectives in order to strengthen
Livingstone’s resilience to climate change while supporting sustainable urban development and
tourism growth.

2. 0 Strategic Rationale
Climate change presents both risks and opportunities for Livingstone City. Without proactive
action, rising temperatures, water scarcity, waste accumulation, ecosystem degradation, and
vector proliferation threaten public health, livelihoods, and tourism competitiveness.
Conversely, climate-responsive planning can improve service delivery, reduce costs, enhance
liveability, and position Livingstone as a leading green tourism city in the region.
This Strategy responds to:
 Increasing pressure on water resources and sanitation systems
 Persistent challenges in solid waste management
 Loss of tree cover and limited green spaces
 Climate-sensitive public and environmental health risks, including mosquito nuisance
 The need to integrate climate considerations into urban planning and infrastructure

3.0 Livingstone City Climate Risk and Vulnerability Profile
3.1 Key Climate Stressors
As a tourist and growing city, Livingstone’s growing and transient population also means
growing demands for land, water, energy and other resources, however, with the effects of
growing urbanisation, climate change effects are even more evident, in the recent past due to
climate variability, the water levels in these rivers has reduced, and some perennial streams
have dried up. This has contributed to the low water table and over-reliance on groundwater.
Further, the energy sector has been adversely affected as the city just as the country level,
depends on hydroelectricity. In the absence of stable hydro power residents often resort to using
charcoal and fire hood leading to increased deforestation, environmental degradation, loss of
biodiversity and declining urban tree cover.
The small scale farmers, women and livestock in the rural part of the district rely on these
streams for agriculture purposes and domestic use. This affects crop production thus, having a
negative impact on their income and indirectly makes households vulnerable to climate change.
Additionally, as a tourist city tourism has long been the backbone of Livingstone’s economy
and the city and the Victoria Falls area remains the biggest attraction in the country and is
widely known for adventure tourism (Nchito & Sichimwa, 2024). Therefore, climate stresses
such as rising average temperatures, heat waves, rainfall variability and prolonged dry spells
affect the sector and also contributes to enhanced human wild life conflicts as some wildlife
such as Elephants, Baboons and Hippos stray into the residential areas to look for food.
Other factors contributing to the key climate stressors in the city are intense rainfall events
causing localised flooding leading to damages to infrastructure such as roads, bridges and
buildings. The city also faces challenges of inadequate solid waste management and illegal
dumping,

7.0 Conclusion
The Livingstone City Climate Change and Resilience Strategy provides a locally grounded
roadmap for addressing climate risks while promoting sustainable urban development. Its
successful implementation will strengthen public health, protect tourism assets, improve
service delivery, and enhance the resilience and liveability of Livingstone City. The Local
Authority recognises the importance of stakeholder involvement in implementing low-carbon
energy developments in the city and the successful implementation of this strategy.
